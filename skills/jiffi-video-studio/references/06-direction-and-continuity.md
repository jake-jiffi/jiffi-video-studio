# Direction and continuity

Read after the concept is chosen, before staging or generating shot keyframes.
Apply this to shots in one continuous scene, including direct/static shots.
Scale it to the subject: a product, landscape, animation or screen workflow
may have no actors, eyelines or physical prop handoffs. Mark irrelevant checks
not applicable. Planned changes of time, location or graphic state are allowed;
make them understandable rather than forcing spatial continuity across them.
This is an internal working check, not another user approval gate.

## Stage one scene

Use `assets/scene-plan-template.md` to create `brief/scene-plan.md`.
When spatial relationships matter, record a simple overhead map: fixed landmarks, action target, actor starting
positions, movement paths and camera positions. Give actors enough room for
the action and readable silhouettes. Their relationships and intentions should
explain where they stand. Do not bunch everyone together to fit a portrait crop.

Choose the line of action and keep coverage on a consistent side by default.
Record who looks towards whom. A deliberate crossing needs a visible movement
or re-establishing view that lets the audience understand the new geography.
For three people, check the active pair and the room map at each cut rather
than treating one left/right label as sufficient for the entire scene.

Lock a master view after blocking. Generate or build closer views from that
same arrangement and its approved references. Character sheets preserve
identity, not room geometry, prop ownership or blocking. A shared lighting
description is not evidence that separately generated rooms match.

## Direct believable performance

Apply this even to a standalone character shot with no continuity problem.

Give each visible character a reason to be in the scene: what they want, what
holds their attention, how they feel and what changes their response. Include
listeners and background participants. A character waiting on a mark still
perceives and responds to events. Choose a few specific behaviours that fit
the person, action and style, rather than just requesting "natural movement".

Let attention lead a motivated reach or turn. Where appropriate, show weight
transfer, preparation, effort, contact, release and recovery. A listener might
track the object, glance at the speaker or adjust their stance after a result.
Use individual timing and restrained expression; identical nods, continuous
swaying, random gestures and synchronised idle loops look artificial. Breathing
and blinking alone do not establish engagement. Respect the character's body,
mobility and intended behaviour. Deliberate stillness, sleep or a stylised held
pose can be correct; do not add motion merely to fill every moment.

Separate what must remain fixed from performance freedom. Lock the necessary
world marks, camera, prop ownership, contact intervals and action outcome.
Permit suitable gaze changes, facial reactions and small postural adjustments
without drifting marks, breaking a grip, changing the result or obscuring it.
Decide whether each important performance beat comes from the 3D animation,
generation prompt or supplied footage. Avoid conflicting instructions from a
rigid guide and a prompt that asks for freer acting. For direct generation,
give the same intentions and event-driven reactions without inventing a 3D step.

Review the whole performance in motion, including people who are not speaking
or handling the main object. Check attention, timing, weight, reaction and
settling. Reject unintended mannequin-like stillness and procedural fidgeting.
Keep performance, visual realism and continuity verdicts separate: realistic
skin cannot make an inert performance believable, and accurate blocking alone
does not establish the best visual outcome. An improved prompt is unproven
until a new take demonstrates the intended performance.

When eye contact matters, name the actual target and account for relative
height and position. Looking towards a face is not proof of looking into its
eyes. Check pupil direction at the peak reaction and during recovery: raised
brows, widened eyes or a lifted chin can accidentally shift attention above
the other person's eyes. Compare enlarged before/after frames as well as the
shot at its intended viewing size. Do not erase the emotional response merely
to make a gaze technically correct; choose other facial or bodily cues when
the original expression conflicts with the target.

Disambiguate an important intention through a short, motivated sequence of
behaviour. For example, looking upward can read as interest or admiration,
even when the prompt says embarrassment. A stimulus, a brief social check,
and one character-specific response may communicate the intention more clearly.
Use only the actions the scene needs. If clothing or a prop is involved, show
support, contact and the resulting movement. Check the actual selected interval
contains the meaningful action; a generated gesture may occur much later than
requested. A held action can be a valid cut point when its result already reads.

## Control what the audience learns

When the concept depends on a reveal, misunderstanding or discovery, record
what the audience can see and infer before and after each beat. A beautiful
wide shot can spoil a later reveal. Plan the required glance, response or
contact before choosing where to cut, and keep the information the story
withholds outside the framing until it is needed.

Judge dramatic actions at the intended viewing size. A prompt for restraint
must not make a pivotal reaction unreadable. Specify the stimulus, change in
attention, response and recovery, with enough variation in timing between
people to avoid mechanical synchronisation. Escalate performance only where
the brief and user feedback call for it. Comedy is one application, not the
default genre.

## Track object and action changes

For every story prop, record its owner, hand or support, location and condition
at the beginning and end of each shot. Distinguish visible, occluded and outside
the frame. A prop outside the frame still exists. Show or motivate a pickup,
handoff or relocation before a later shot depends on it. Remove an unnecessary
prop from all shots if that makes the scene clearer.

For each meaningful action, state the visible result that proves it happened.
A handoff needs a coherent transfer and final owner; a software demonstration
needs the actual changed UI state; a physical intervention needs its intended
effect. Movement towards a target does not prove success. Preserve the stable
facts of the setting while tracking the change the audience must understand.

## Choose coverage for the beat

Give every shot a purpose and a reason to cut: establish a relationship, reveal
an object, show an action, or catch a reaction. Vary shot size when useful while
preserving perspective, eyelines and established geography. Matching continuity
does not mean using identical framing for every shot.

When someone indicates an object, show the gaze or gesture and its target
clearly, or follow with a motivated insert. Frame the full interaction path,
including the hand and the landing. Hold the result long enough to register
before the reaction or punchline. Do not hide the proof with captions.

Choose aspect-ratio coverage before rendering. If a shared composition crowds
actors or loses the action, plan a wider picture window or separate coverage.
An object barely inside the crop is not necessarily legible on a phone.

## Rehearse before spending on the sequence

Review the planned sequence in edit order. Use the proportional method chosen
in `07-storyboarding.md`: existing source frames or an edit list may suffice.
If using boards, include important result frames, not just shot starts. Mark
each requested output shape and check state on both sides of each cut. Stills
can establish composition but cannot validate an interaction.

When an uncertain generated interaction puts dependent shots at risk, render
it as a short pilot within the existing budget
before launching dependent shots. Inspect approach, contact, release and the
settled result. If it fails, change the blocking or coverage before adding more
subjects, camera motion or other complexity. Do not batch dependent shots
while their geography or action outcome is unresolved. Reuse the accepted
pilot's actual end state when planning the next shot, if that frame is suitable.

## Review the sequence

Compare the outgoing and incoming frames at every cut. Check who occupies each
part of the room, where they look, which hand holds the prop and its position,
the action phase, scale and background landmarks. For motion faults, inspect
the relevant interval densely or in playback, not just one frame per second.
Judge story readability with sound and captions off as well as in the final cut.

Reject a take or cut that breaks a story-critical state or fails to show the
required outcome. Trimming is a repair only when the needed result survives
the trim. A nicer crop, matched wardrobe or successful decode cannot rescue a
missing action. Record observed evidence and unresolved questions separately.

Keep technical, continuity and storytelling verdicts separate in the review.
If the user identifies a continuity failure, mark the creative verdict failed,
preserve the rejected cut and correct earlier review claims. Do not leave it
labelled merely as awaiting playback. A revised instruction is an unproven
process improvement until a new sequence demonstrates it.
