# Sound bible and spotting

Use for an uncertain soundtrack, a changing emotional arc or recurring sonic
identity. For a short edit, a paragraph and cue row may suffice. Longer work
can share one bible with sequence cue sheets. Silent work can skip this.
Use `assets/sound-plan-template.md` within the existing brief and review gates.

## Plan the experience before choosing a track

Review the whole script or available edit, including pauses, transitions and
the ending. Record which picture ranges were inspected and which audio was
actually heard. Samples and transcripts do not establish a playback review.

State what the audience should feel and understand at each turning point.
Choose whether sound supports, contrasts with or withholds information from
the image. Explain any contrast so it remains intentional. Identify whose
experience the audience hears, including off-screen space when relevant.
Do not equate a camera crop with microphone distance.

Record a compact sound bible: emotional arc, acoustic viewpoint, voice rules,
palette, rhythmic activity, density, recurring motifs, references and things
to avoid. Use descriptors with an audible consequence, such as sustained
texture versus a tune, rounded versus sharp attacks, sparse versus busy
events. A genre label alone is not a sufficient brief.

## Spot the edit

Assign each cue an in/out range or event trigger, dramatic job, lead sound,
supporting layers, important sync points and ending behaviour. Select where
music should enter, develop, recede or be absent. Avoid automatic music on
every cut, impact on every gesture or sound effect on every visual animation.

Preserve useful pauses and natural room tone. Distinguish no music from an
unnaturally empty acoustic setting. Let dialogue, a physical event, ambience
or music lead as the moment requires. A beautiful isolated track can still
be wrong for the scene. Consider arrangement and competing frequency ranges
as well as volume when a mix feels crowded.

## Resolve taste with small, controlled comparisons

Ask two or three questions about unresolved decisions, using existing answers:
the intended feeling, how noticeable the music should be, and a useful
reference or a specific dislike. Explain options in everyday language.
Request an exact reference interval and what to borrow from it. If level
feedback varies, establish the listening device and keep player volume fixed.

Separate level, palette, rhythm, entrance timing and dramatic fit. Change one
dimension at a time when diagnosing a rejection. A quieter same-cue version
tests level only. A no-music control can reveal whether a cue contributes
anything; preserve other relevant scene sound. When useful and authorised,
audition two or three short, distinct directions against the same picture
at comparable perceived loudness. Check the transition into the excerpt too.

After selection, refine the mix and review the full export on the intended
playback route, with a mono check where relevant. Check section loudness,
peaks, noise-floor continuity and intelligibility as technical evidence.
No meter value establishes taste or emotional fit. If listening is unavailable,
record user descriptions and proposals honestly. Do not replace a request
for style discussion with repeated speculative paid generations.

Where authorised, [audio and video review](12-audio-video-review.md) adds a
source-audio description and a separate assessment against the creative brief.
Check whether the generated result actually contains the requested palette,
including unwanted vocals, before building a full mix. Do not promote prompt
intentions into descriptions of the delivered sound.

## Established references and limits of this research

These are foundations, not universal recipes. The workflow above is this
studio's practical synthesis. Links and descriptions were checked on
25 September 2026. Book descriptions are not a claim to have read the books.

- **Michel Chion, Audio-Vision: Sound on Screen, second edition.** The
  [publisher's description and contents](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/audio-vision-sound-on-screen/9780231185899/)
  frame sound and image as a combined perceptual experience. Useful for
  analysing how a soundtrack changes the reading of an image. Full book
  not accessed in this research.
- **Randy Thom, Designing a Movie for Sound.** His
  [1999 essay](https://www.filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm)
  argues for sound to influence the script and edit, using time, space and
  point of view. The authored essay was read through its FilmSound reprint.
- **Randy Thom, A Few Notes on Music in the Final Mix.** This
  [authored account](https://www.filmsound.org/randythom/finalmix.htm)
  describes joint spotting on Contact and deciding where music or effects
  would lead. Read the reprint, including in Chrome. Historical theatre
  routing and film-reel advice is not a modern mobile-delivery specification.
- **Walter Murch, Stretching Sound to Help the Mind See.** The
  [authored essay](https://www.filmsound.org/murch/stretching.htm)
  explores selective omission and the expressive relationship between sound
  and image. Read the reprint. Intentional counterpoint is a creative option,
  not an excuse for accidental mismatch.
- **David Sonnenschein, Sound Design: The Expressive Power of Music, Voice,
  and Sound Effects in Cinema.** The
  [publisher's catalogue, printed page 44](https://mwp.mwp.com/catalogs/MWP.com_Catalog_2013.pdf#page=44)
  describes an integrated approach to music, voice, effects and emotion.
  Bibliographic scope verified; full book not accessed.
- **Ric Viers, The Sound Effects Bible and The Location Sound Bible.** The
  same publisher's catalogue describes effects recording/editing on printed
  page 44 and location dialogue recording on printed page 11. These are
  production-craft references, not music-selection formulas. Full books
  not accessed; do not infer modern equipment recommendations from the old
  catalogue or purchase anything merely because it is listed here.
